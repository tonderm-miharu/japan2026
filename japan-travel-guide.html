Culture & Tradition

The Art of Onsen

Discovering Japan's sacred bathing culture across volcanic landscapes

Japan sits atop the Pacific Ring of Fire, blessed with over 27,000 natural hot springs. For centuries, the Japanese have transformed these geothermal gifts into sanctuaries of relaxation, healing, and spiritual renewal.

The onsen tradition dates back to the 8th century, when Buddhist monks first recognized the therapeutic properties of mineral-rich waters. What began as medicinal practice evolved into an essential part of Japanese culture - a ritual that strips away social barriers along with one's clothes, creating spaces of pure equality and tranquility.

Beppu - Hell on Earth

With eight distinct "hells" (jigoku) and more hot spring sources than anywhere else in Japan, Beppu is the country's onsen capital. Steam rises from streets, cooking pits bubble with geothermal heat, and locals relax in sand baths along the beach. The city produces enough hot water to fill 100 Olympic pools daily.

Kurokawa Onsen

Hidden in the mountains of Kumamoto, this intimate onsen village offers the tegata system - a wooden pass granting access to three different ryokan baths. Lantern-lit paths connect traditional inns, each with its own character: cave baths, riverside rotenburo, and forest-view pools.

"In an onsen, rank and status dissolve in the steam. A CEO soaks beside a student, a tourist beside a monk. The water makes us equal."

Unzen Jigoku

The "hells" of Unzen are more than tourist attractions - they're reminders of nature's power. Christian martyrs were boiled in these sulfurous pools during the 17th century persecutions. Today, the landscape of bubbling mud and hissing steam offers both spectacle and excellent hot spring hotels.

Onsen Etiquette

  • Wash thoroughly before entering the bath
  • Never wear swimwear (unless specified)
  • Keep your small towel out of the water
  • Don't swim or splash
  • Tattoos may restrict access at traditional onsen
  • Stay hydrated - limit soaking to 15-20 minutes

Adventure

Shimanami Kaido

70 kilometers across the Seto Inland Sea - Japan's most spectacular cycling route

Connecting Honshu to Shikoku across six islands, the Shimanami Kaido is more than a bridge - it's a journey through maritime history, artistic villages, and some of Japan's most breathtaking coastal scenery.

Completed in 1999, this expressway is unique in Japan for having dedicated cycling and pedestrian paths that spiral up to each bridge. Cyclists glide alongside massive suspension cables, hundreds of meters above shipping lanes where container ships pass silently below.

The Route

Starting from Onomichi - a hillside town of temples, cats, and nostalgic alleys - the route crosses Mukaishima, Innoshima, Ikuchijima, Omishima, Hakatajima, and Oshima before reaching Imabari on Shikoku. Each island has its own character: citrus groves, ancient shrines, contemporary art, and fishing villages where time moves slowly.

"Below me, the Seto Inland Sea spread like hammered silver. Above, clouds drifted through suspension cables. I was cycling through the sky."

Ikuchijima: Art & History

Home to Kosanji Temple - a millionaire's tribute to his mother featuring replicas of famous Japanese buildings - and the stunning Hill of Hope, where marble sculptures overlook the sea. The nearby Shiomachi shopping street preserves Edo-period charm with local treats and cycling cafes.

Omishima: Sacred Island

Oyamazumi Shrine has stood here for over 2,600 years, housing Japan's largest collection of samurai armor and weapons. The ancient camphor trees create an atmosphere of profound peace. Stay overnight at SANDO, a modern guesthouse that perfectly bridges tradition and contemporary design.

The Bridges

Each of the six bridges offers a different perspective. The Kurushima Kaikyo Bridge - the world's longest suspension bridge span over a navigable strait - provides the journey's climax. At 4 kilometers long, it feels like cycling across an ocean.

Practical Tips

  • Rent bikes in Onomichi (Giant Store or Shimanami Rental)
  • Reserve 2 days for the full experience
  • Follow the blue line painted on the road
  • Ship luggage ahead via Yamato (TA-Q-BIN)
  • Best seasons: Spring (March-May) or Autumn (Oct-Nov)
  • Don't miss: Salt ice cream on Hakatajima

This Journey's Shimanami Plan

Day 19 (Oct 6): Onomichi → Omishima (~50km). Onomichi's temple walk, ferry to Mukaishima, Kosanji Temple, Hill of Hope, overnight at SANDO.

Day 20 (Oct 7): Omishima → Imabari → Matsuyama (~45km). Oyamazumi Shrine at dawn, Hakatajima beach, Kirosan Observatory (Kengo Kuma architecture), Kurushima Bridge finale, train to Matsuyama.

History

Sakamoto Ryoma

Following the footsteps of Japan's most beloved revolutionary samurai

He was assassinated at 31, but Sakamoto Ryoma accomplished more in his short life than most historical figures manage in a lifetime. His vision helped end 250 years of samurai rule and launched Japan into the modern era.

Born in 1836 in Tosa (now Kochi Prefecture), Ryoma was a low-ranking samurai who became a ronin, a rebel, a fugitive, and ultimately a visionary who brokered the alliance that toppled the Tokugawa shogunate. He negotiated Japan's first modern trading company, drafted an early version of the constitution, and somehow found time to practice swordsmanship and write poetry.

The Irohamaru Incident

In 1867, Ryoma's ship Irohamaru collided with a vessel from the powerful Kishu domain near Tomonoura. Rather than accept blame, Ryoma demanded compensation - and won. Using international maritime law (radical for Japan at the time), he secured payment that funded his revolutionary activities. The Irohamaru Exhibition in Tomonoura tells this remarkable story.

"I will accomplish what I set out to do, or die trying. There is no other path for a man."
- Sakamoto Ryoma

Nagasaki: The Revolutionary Base

Nagasaki was Japan's window to the world, and Ryoma made it his base. He founded the Kameyama Shachu here - often called Japan's first modern company - trading goods and weapons while secretly working to unite rival domains against the shogunate. His statue gazes over the city from Kazagashira Park, and his famous boots monument celebrates his embrace of Western ways.

A Modern Hero

Japanese people consistently rank Ryoma among history's most admired figures. He represents the possibility of change - a low-ranking outsider who transformed a nation through intelligence, charisma, and sheer determination. His image appears on products, in manga, and in countless historical dramas.

Ryoma Sites on This Journey

  • Tomonoura (Oct 5): Irohamaru Exhibition, Masuya Seiemon hideout
  • Nagasaki (Oct 19): Kameyama Shachu, Ryoma's Boots, Kazagashira statue
  • Kochi (not on route): Birthplace and museum

What makes Ryoma compelling is his humanity. He wasn't just a warrior - he was a dreamer who imagined a Japan connected to the world, where talent mattered more than birth, where old enemies could become allies. That vision cost him his life in 1867, but it shaped the nation that emerged.

Alternative Adventure

Aogashima

The world's most remote volcanic caldera - and the hardest place in Japan to reach

Imagine a volcanic island within a volcanic island, inhabited by fewer than 170 people, accessible only by helicopter (when weather permits) or a ferry that cancels more often than it runs. This is Aogashima - Japan's most challenging destination.

Rising from the Philippine Sea some 360 kilometers south of Tokyo, Aogashima is a double caldera: an outer rim surrounding an inner cone called Maruyama. The 1785 eruption killed nearly half the population and rendered the island uninhabitable for 50 years. Today, residents live on the caldera's inner slopes, cooking food in geothermal vents and enjoying some of Japan's clearest night skies.

Getting There: The Challenge

There are exactly two ways to reach Aogashima: a ferry from Hachijojima that cancels in rough seas (about 40% of attempts fail in autumn), or a 9-seat helicopter that books out within minutes. The helicopter operates just once daily, and tickets go on sale exactly one month in advance at 9:00 AM Japan time. Refreshing the booking page becomes an obsession.

"As the helicopter crested the outer rim, the inner caldera revealed itself - a green bowl with a perfect cone rising from its center. I understood then why people call this place prehistoric."

What Awaits

Oyama Viewpoint: The main attraction - views down into the double caldera that seem pulled from another planet.

Maruyama Crater: A 40-minute hike into the inner volcano, where steam vents and the smell of sulfur remind you this place is very much alive.

Sauna Jigoku: A natural geothermal sauna built into the rock - free, primitive, unforgettable.

Stargazing: Zero light pollution means the Milky Way blazes overhead. Some visitors come just for this.

The Backup Reality

The honest truth: there's about a 30% chance the helicopter tickets won't be available. Autumn weather adds another 20% cancellation risk. This is not a journey for the inflexible. But for those willing to gamble, the reward is standing on one of Earth's most extraordinary landscapes.

Aogashima Contingency Plan

  • Sep 27, 9:00 JP: Attempt heli booking Oct 27 (outbound)
  • Sep 28, 9:00 JP: Attempt heli booking Oct 28 (return)
  • If success: Book ANA flights, minshuku, proceed with island adventure
  • If failure: Default to Izu Peninsula road trip (Mt. Omuro, Kawazu, Hottarakashi)
  • Hotel on Hachijojima already booked as backup (Noboryuso, cancel free until Oct 20)

Logistics

Accommodation: Minshuku Kazutomo (04996-9-0033) or Nagomi (04996-9-0066). Call in Japanese. Expect simple rooms, included dinner of local fish and shochu.

Budget: ~720 EUR total including flights, helicopter, 3 nights on Hachijojima, 1 night on Aogashima.

Essential: Cash only on Aogashima. Bring snacks. Download offline maps.
