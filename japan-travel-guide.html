Culture & Tradition

The Art of Onsen

Japan's sacred bathing culture across volcanic landscapes

Japan sits atop the Pacific Ring of Fire, blessed with over 27,000 natural hot springs. The onsen tradition dates back to the 8th century, creating sanctuaries of relaxation where social barriers dissolve along with one's clothes.

Beppu: Hell on Earth

With eight distinct "hells" (jigoku) and more hot spring sources than anywhere else in Japan, Beppu produces enough hot water to fill 100 Olympic pools daily. Steam rises from streets, cooking pits bubble with geothermal heat, and the sulfurous air signals you've entered Japan's onsen capital.

The Kannawa district offers the most concentrated onsen experience, with public baths ranging from simple neighborhood sento to elaborate facilities with multiple pools at different temperatures. Don't miss jigoku-mushi: food steamed in volcanic vents.

Kurokawa Onsen

Hidden in Kumamoto's mountains, this intimate village offers the tegata system — a wooden pass granting access to three different ryokan baths. Lantern-lit paths connect traditional inns with cave baths, riverside rotenburo, and forest-view pools. Each ryokan maintains its own character: some carved into cliffsides, others overlooking rushing streams.

"In an onsen, rank and status dissolve in the steam. A CEO soaks beside a student. The water makes us equal."

Unzen: Volcanic Inferno

Shimabara Peninsula's volcanic hotspot offers otherworldly landscapes of steaming vents and bubbling mud pools. Walk through the Unzen Jigoku (Unzen Hell) before retreating to your ryokan's private rotenburo overlooking the apocalyptic scenery.

Hottarakashi: Mountain-Top Dawn

Perched high above Kofu in Yamanashi Prefecture, this rustic onsen offers what many consider Japan's finest sunrise view. Arrive before dawn, soak in the outdoor pools as the first light hits Mt. Fuji's snow-capped peak. The name means "left alone" — a reference to its deliberately undeveloped character.

Onsen Etiquette

  • Wash thoroughly at the shower stations before entering any pool
  • Never wear swimwear in traditional onsen (some tourist spots allow it)
  • Keep your small towel out of the water — place it on your head or poolside
  • Don't swim, splash, or submerge your head
  • Tattoos may restrict access — research tattoo-friendly facilities
  • Limit soaking to 15-20 minutes to avoid overheating
  • Hydrate well — the minerals and heat are dehydrating

Adventure

Shimanami Kaido

70 kilometers across the Seto Inland Sea — Japan's most spectacular cycling route

Connecting Honshu to Shikoku across six islands, the Shimanami Kaido is more than a bridge system — it's a journey through maritime history, artistic villages, citrus groves, and breathtaking coastal scenery that unfolds at bicycle pace.

The Route

Starting from the hillside town of Onomichi, the route crosses Mukaishima, Innoshima, Ikuchijima, Omishima, Hakatajima, and Oshima before reaching Imabari on Shikoku. Each island offers its own character: citrus farms scenting the air with yuzu and mikan, ancient shrines housing samurai treasures, contemporary art installations, and fishing villages where time moves at the rhythm of the tides.

A blue line painted on the road serves as your guide, leading you from bridge to bridge. The cycling path is separated from vehicle traffic, making it safe and meditative.

Ikuchijima: Art & Devotion

Home to Kosanji Temple, a millionaire's tribute to his mother featuring replicas of famous buildings from across Japan — Nikko's Yomeimon Gate, Byodo-in's Phoenix Hall — all rendered in brilliant color. Above the temple, the Hill of Hope offers stunning marble sculptures overlooking the island-dotted sea. The Shiomachi shopping street below preserves Showa-era charm.

"The bridges are the punctuation marks. The islands are the sentences. Together they tell the story of the Inland Sea."

Omishima: Sacred Island

Oyamazumi Shrine has stood for over 2,600 years, housing Japan's largest collection of samurai armor and weapons — 80% of the nationally designated treasures in this category. The shrine's camphor trees are over 2,500 years old. Sleep at SANDO, a beautifully converted guesthouse bridging tradition and contemporary design.

The Bridges

Each of the seven bridges offers different perspectives: the elegant curves of Tatara (once the longest cable-stayed span), the dramatic triple suspension of Kurushima Kaikyo connecting three islands at once. From the dedicated cycling paths, you're eye-level with the cables, the sea spread beneath you.

Practical Tips

  • Rent bikes at Giant Store Onomichi (advance booking for multi-day essential)
  • Reserve two full days for a relaxed experience with time to explore
  • Ship main luggage ahead via Yamato to your final hotel
  • Follow the blue painted line — it never lies
  • Kirosan Observatory (Kengo Kuma design) rewards a steep 3km climb
  • Salt ice cream on Hakatajima is the perfect mid-route reward
  • Wind typically blows from Onomichi to Imabari — ride with it if possible

History

Sakamoto Ryoma

Following the revolutionary who transformed Japan

Sakamoto Ryoma (1836-1867) was a visionary samurai who helped overthrow the Tokugawa shogunate and modernize Japan. Assassinated at 31, he never saw the Meiji Restoration he made possible. This journey traces his footsteps from Nagasaki to the Inland Sea.

Who Was Ryoma?

Born to a lower-ranking samurai family in Tosa (modern Kochi), Ryoma defied class barriers and domain loyalties. He envisioned a unified Japan with a modern navy and democratic government — radical ideas in feudal times. He founded Japan's first trading company, brokered the alliance between rival domains that toppled the shogunate, and drafted an eight-point plan that became the template for Meiji reforms.

Ryoma famously carried both a sword and a revolver, symbolizing his embrace of Western technology while honoring Japanese tradition. He was assassinated in Kyoto just weeks before the Restoration succeeded.

"Once you make a decision, the universe conspires to make it happen." — Sakamoto Ryoma

Tomonoura: The Irohamaru Incident

This picturesque port town witnessed a pivotal moment. In 1867, Ryoma's ship Irohamaru collided with a Kishu domain vessel and sank. Using international maritime law — unprecedented in Japan — Ryoma successfully demanded compensation. The Irohamaru Exhibition Hall now tells this story, while the Joyato lighthouse that guided ships then still stands.

The town also inspired Studio Ghibli's "Ponyo" — Miyazaki based its harbor setting on Tomonoura's timeless atmosphere.

Nagasaki: The Trading Post

Ryoma established the Kameyama Shachu here — Japan's first modern company — trading weapons and ships to fund the revolution. The winding streets of the Teramachi temple district preserve the atmosphere of the port city where East met West. Visit Ryoma's bronze boots at Kazagashira Park, his watchpost with harbor views.

Ryoma Sites on This Journey

  • Oct 5 Tomonoura: Irohamaru Exhibition, Joyato lighthouse, Masuya hideout
  • Oct 19 Nagasaki: Kameyama Shachu headquarters, Ryoma-dori street
  • Oct 19 Nagasaki: Kazagashira Park statue with bronze boots
  • Oct 19 Nagasaki: Kofukuji Temple area, Teramachi district

Ryoma's Legacy

Why does Ryoma remain so beloved 150+ years later? Perhaps because he represents possibility — a low-ranking samurai who changed history through vision, diplomacy, and daring. He appears in countless manga, films, and video games. NHK's 2010 Taiga drama "Ryomaden" sparked renewed interest in his story.

Walking where he walked, you sense both the beauty that inspired him and the dangers he faced. Every harbor, every temple district holds echoes of Japan's dramatic transformation from feudal isolation to modern nation.

The Bakumatsu Period

The final years of the Tokugawa shogunate (1853-1868) saw Japan convulsed by crisis. American "Black Ships" demanded trading rights. Assassinations and street battles became common. Feudal domains competed for influence. From this chaos emerged figures like Ryoma who glimpsed a new Japan — one that would become the industrial power of the early 20th century.

Alternative Adventure

Aogashima

Japan's most remote inhabited volcanic caldera

A volcanic island within a volcanic island, inhabited by approximately 170 people, accessible only by helicopter when weather permits or by a ferry that cancels more often than it runs. Aogashima is Japan's hardest destination to reach — and among its most otherworldly.

Double Caldera

Aogashima's defining feature is its nested volcanic structure: a main caldera containing a secondary cone called Maruyama. The 1785 eruption killed nearly half the population; survivors evacuated to Hachijojima and didn't return for 50 years. Today, steam still vents from the inner crater, and residents use geothermal heat to cook and distill shochu.

Getting There: The Challenge

The 9-seat helicopter from Hachijojima books out within minutes of release. Tickets go on sale exactly one month in advance at 9:00 AM Japan time. The ferry, while cheaper, cancels 40-50% of attempts during autumn due to rough seas and the island's treacherous harbor.

This uncertainty is part of Aogashima's allure. You can't simply arrive — you must earn your passage through planning, luck, and patience.

The Contingency Plan

  • Sep 27, 9:00 JP: Attempt helicopter booking Oct 27 outbound
  • Sep 28, 9:00 JP: Attempt helicopter booking Oct 28 return

  • If failure: Default to Izu Peninsula road trip (already researched)
  • Hachijojima hotel already booked (Noboryuso, cancel free until Oct 20)

What Awaits

Oyama Viewpoint: The main observation deck offers views down into the double caldera that seem from another planet. Green slopes, steaming vents, the inner cone rising from the crater floor.

Maruyama Crater: A 40-minute hike into the inner volcano, where steam emerges from fissures and the smell of sulfur fills the air. The island's geothermal activity is palpable here.

Sauna Jigoku: A natural geothermal sauna built into the rock face. Free, primitive, unforgettable. Bring your own towel; the experience is DIY.

Stargazing: With zero light pollution — no street lights, few buildings — the Milky Way is visible to the naked eye. The stars here are the stars our ancestors knew.

Logistics

The island has no restaurants — meals come from your minshuku. Cash only; no ATMs. Bring supplies, snacks, and offline maps. Cell service is limited but exists. The single shop stocks basics.

Budget: ~720 EUR total (flights, helicopter both ways, 3 nights Hachijojima, 1 night Aogashima minshuku)

大阪
Osaka
September 19-26

Japan's kitchen and commercial heart. Where merchants built fortunes, comedians honed their craft, and the Danjiri festival sends massive wooden floats careening through narrow streets. Osaka rewards those who eat first and sightsee later.

Danjiri Matsuri

Each September, Kishiwada erupts in controlled chaos. Teams of young men haul 4-ton wooden floats at running speed through tight corners in a ritual called yarimawashi. The evening tomoshibi parade transforms floats into glowing lanterns. This is Japan's most dangerous — and exhilarating — festival.

Yokagura: Dance of the Gods

This ancient Shinto ritual has been performed in Takachiho for over 800 years. Masked dancers enact mythological episodes: Amaterasu emerging from her cave, deities descending to earth. The nightly tourist performance at Takachiho Shrine shows excerpts; full village ceremonies run all night during winter festivals.

The subtropical finale. Former Ryukyu Kingdom, now Japan's beach paradise. From Miyakojima's turquoise lagoons to sacred Sefa Utaki, Okinawa offers a different Japan: slower, warmer, shaped by Chinese, Southeast Asian, and American influences.

The Ryukyu Kingdom

For 450 years (1429-1879), the Ryukyu Kingdom was an independent nation bridging China and Japan. Its legacy lives in sacred groves called utaki, the distinctive red-tiled architecture, lion-dog shisa guardians, and a cuisine featuring pork, bitter melon, and tropical fruits unknown on the mainland.
